# Can a derived surface decide a drainage fall?

This is a gate rather than a description. The mechanism being right does not make the answer
usable: a surface derived from shots at real survey density either resolves the decision
somebody collected those shots for, or it does not, and which it is has to be measured
against a requirement stated *before* the surface is built rather than settled afterwards to
fit whatever came out.

The requirement is stated in the first section below, the arithmetic in the second, and the
measurement in the third. It runs against [`testdata/surface/patio`](../testdata/surface/patio),
which is checked in, and the outcome is recorded in
[`testdata/surface/patio/decision.txt`](../testdata/surface/patio/decision.txt), which is
regenerated by `go test . -update` and compared byte for byte in CI. A change to the
propagation which moves the finding is therefore a diff somebody has to explain.

**The finding, up front: it misses, by a factor of four — and survey density is not the
reason.** The instrument is. No density of GNSS RTK shots meets this requirement, and the
same ground at the same density levelled instead meets it with room to spare.

## The decision, stated first

A patio outside a back door has to fall away from the house or it does not drain. The
specification is one in eighty, and the ground under the question is four metres of run from
the threshold to the drain edge — fifty millimetres of drop if it was laid to what it was
specified at. Too steep is a defect of its own: past about one in forty a paved surface is
unpleasant to stand furniture on.

So the decision is made between two grades fifty millimetres apart over that run, and what
the survey has to do is resolve the fall well enough that the decision turns on the patio
rather than on the survey:

> **The fall from the threshold to the drain edge — four metres of run — must be known to a
> standard uncertainty of 5 mm (k = 1), which is 10 mm at k = 2.**

Ten millimetres at 95% is a fifth of the gap between the two grades being decided between.
That is the whole of the reasoning, and it is the kind of reasoning which has to be written
down before the answer is known, because "near enough" after the fact is not a requirement —
it is a description of whatever the survey happened to achieve.

Two further figures were stated at the same time, both of them properties of the job rather
than of any file:

| Stated | Figure | Why it cannot be read out of the model |
|---|---|---|
| How far the ground departs from the interpolation | 0.003 m per metre from the nearest shot | A laid slab and a ploughed field sampled on the same grid give identical arithmetic and are not known equally well. Nothing in a set of shots implies it. |
| What one occupation on one base station is worth | 0.010 m (rover), 0.003 m (level run) | An observation record states the precision the instrument reported for *that shot*. What the setup was worth is a judgment made afterwards, by whoever reduced the session. |

Both are stated on the derivation ([`SurfaceDerivation.Roughness`](../surface.go),
`SurfaceDerivation.Systematic`), which puts them on the result and in the cache key like every
other parameter, rather than in a constant nobody can read back off the answer.

## What the survey was

Twenty-five shots on a 1.5 m by 1.25 m grid over a seven-by-six-metre patio, in one
occupation. That is a dense survey of a patio by any measure — a crew is not going to stand
on twenty-five marks for a patio twice — and it is the density the gate is decided at.

The fixture holds the same ground twice, ten metres apart, at the same density, surveyed by
two instruments: `site:S-patio` on a GNSS rover good to 21 mm in the vertical, and
`site:S-patio-levelled` on a level good to 2 mm. That is the whole design of it. Whatever
differs between the two answers cannot be the density, because the density is identical.

Every shot of both falls exactly on the plane `z = 100 + 0.0125y`, so what a level *is* stays
arithmetic a test can state. The fixture is not about residuals; it is about what the answer
is worth.

## The arithmetic

Three rules, and they are the reason this is a model rather than an interchange format
([0006](decisions/0006-accuracy-is-one-sigma.md)).

**Independent errors combine in quadrature, under the weights the interpolation gave them.**
A level from three shots at weights ⅖, 0 and ⅗ costs `√((0.4·σ)² + (0.6·σ)²)` = 0.72σ, not σ
and not σ/√3.

**A term a whole afternoon shares is added linearly and counted once.** A base station set up
over the wrong mark moves every shot of that session the same way. The weights of an
interpolation sum to one, so the term arrives at the level *whole* — 10 mm of base station is
10 mm of level, however many shots were averaged to get there. Counting it once per shot
instead would inflate it; combining it in quadrature would shrink it by √3 and quietly claim
an accuracy nobody has.

**The ground between the shots is charged for separately**, at the stated roughness times the
distance in plan to the nearest shot. It is nought at a shot — a triangulation honours its own
points — and it grows as the question is asked further from the evidence. Where no roughness
is stated the term is absent and [`Elevation.Complete`](../surface.go) reports false, which is
the model saying *this figure is a floor* rather than silently treating unmeasured ground as
perfectly smooth.

Then the part which matters most for a decision about a *fall*: the difference of two levels
is not the two levels combined. `Surface.Fall` accumulates each contributing shot at the
**difference** of the weights the two ends gave it, before taking any absolute value. A shot
behind both ends partially cancels. A session behind both ends cancels exactly — its
coefficient is `1 − 1` — which is why the base station does not appear in the fall at all:

```
    systematic session:2026-06-03-am                 0.0000 m (cancels: it is in both ends by the same amount)
```

A caller who took `Elevation.Uncertainty` twice and combined the two in quadrature would have
counted that error twice over instead of nought times, and would then have gone off to
re-survey ground which was never the problem.

## What was measured

Recorded in full in [`testdata/surface/patio/decision.txt`](../testdata/surface/patio/decision.txt).
The rover survey:

```
site:S-patio — gnss-rtk, 25 shots on a 1.500 m by 1.250 m grid, vertical precision 0.021 m
  the fall is 0.0500 m over 4.000 m of run, which is 1 in 80.0
  known to 0.0215 m (k = 1, ≈ 68%), which is 0.0430 m (k = 2, ≈ 95%)
  each end on its own is worth 0.0182 m
  ...
  achieved 0.0215 m against a requirement of 0.0050 m, 4.3 times it
  verdict: DOES NOT DECIDE
  the shots alone are worth 0.0214 m, so a denser survey cannot reach it
```

**It misses by a factor of 4.3.** The fall itself is right — fifty millimetres, one in eighty,
which is what the ground does — and the uncertainty on it is four times what deciding against
it needs. At 43 mm on a 95% interval, a patio laid dead on one in eighty is indistinguishable
from one laid at one in forty-five or at one in three hundred. The survey cannot tell you
whether the patio drains.

## The finding: density is not the constraint

The issue this gate came from expected the finding to be *the survey density is insufficient,
and here is the density that would be needed*. It is not, and saying so is the point of
measuring rather than assuming.

Of the 21.5 mm budget, the ground between the shots contributes 1.5 mm — two terms of 1.5 mm,
one per end, at half a metre from the nearest shot. Take it out entirely, which is what an
infinitely dense survey with this instrument would buy, and **21.4 mm remains**. That is the
line the record ends with: `the shots alone are worth 0.0214 m, so a denser survey cannot
reach it`.

The reason is structural rather than particular to this patio. A triangulated surface answers
each point from the enclosing facet, so a level always rests on two or three shots whose
weights sum to one, whatever else is on the ground. Adding shots moves the facet corners
closer and shrinks only the interpolation term; it never averages the instrument's error down,
because no extra shot ever enters the answer. On these weights the floor is 0.72σ per end and
1.02σ for the difference — about 21 mm for a 21 mm instrument, at any density whatsoever.

**What would be needed is a better instrument**, and the second half of the fixture measures
exactly that rather than asserting it:

```
site:S-patio-levelled — levelling, 25 shots on a 1.500 m by 1.250 m grid, vertical precision 0.002 m
  known to 0.0029 m (k = 1, ≈ 68%), which is 0.0059 m (k = 2, ≈ 95%)
  achieved 0.0029 m against a requirement of 0.0050 m, 0.6 times it
  verdict: DECIDES
  the shots alone are worth 0.0020 m, so a denser survey reaches it
  it holds out to 1.076 m from the nearest shot, a grid of 1.522 m
```

Same ground, same density, same arithmetic, an instrument ten times better in the vertical:
2.9 mm against a requirement of 5 mm. And at *that* instrument density does start to be the
question — the dominant term of the levelled budget is the interpolation, not the shots. The
requirement holds as long as no point of the patio is more than **1.08 m from a shot**, which
on a square grid is a spacing of **1.52 m**. The 1.5 m by 1.25 m grid surveyed keeps every
point within 0.98 m, so it clears it; a 2 m grid would not.

So the density that would be needed, stated the way the issue asked for it: **levelled shots
at 2 mm, on a grid no coarser than 1.5 m.** Not more RTK shots. Never more RTK shots.

## What this does not claim

- **The roughness is stated, not measured.** 0.003 m per metre is a figure about paving that a
  project asserts; nothing here derives it, and a patio laid worse than that has a budget
  wider than this by exactly the ratio.
- **The two ends' interpolation terms are treated as independent.** How far the ground departs
  from the interpolation under the threshold says nothing here about how far it departs four
  metres away. Over a much shorter run that assumption is pessimistic, and nothing in the
  model states a correlation length to do better with.
- **The fixture's ground is a plane.** That makes every level checkable arithmetic rather than
  a number copied out of a run, and it means the fixture measures the *budget* of an answer
  and never the residual of one. A surface whose interpolation was subtly wrong would be
  caught by the tests beside it, not by this document.
- **The requirement is one project's.** Five millimetres is the figure for deciding a
  drainage fall to this specification over this run. `Fall.Decides` takes it as an argument
  for that reason: what a fall has to be known to is a property of what is being built on it,
  and the engine carries no such vocabulary
  ([0010](decisions/0010-the-engine-carries-no-domain-vocabulary.md)).
